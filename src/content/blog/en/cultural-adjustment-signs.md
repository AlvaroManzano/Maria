You expected the move to take some adjusting. You prepared for the language, the food, the unfamiliar streets. What you may not have expected is how deeply cultural differences would get under your skin, not in the first weeks but in the months and years that followed.

## The U-curve, and why real life isn’t that tidy

Cultural adjustment is often described as a U-shaped curve: the honeymoon phase, then a crisis of frustration and disillusionment, then recovery, and finally adjustment. In real life, many people cycle through these stages again and again, or stay stuck in the crisis phase without realising it. These five signs can help you notice when ordinary discomfort has become something that deserves attention.

## 1. You feel disproportionately angry at everyday things

The shop closed early. The plumber never turned up. Someone skipped the queue. Small things, and yet they fill you with a rage that feels out of proportion: “This would never happen back home.” The anger is rarely about the plumber. It is about the accumulated effort of living in a system with different unwritten rules, where nobody gave you the manual.

## 2. You’ve stopped trying to connect with locals

At first you made an effort. Over time it started to feel exhausting and unrewarding, and you withdrew into expat circles or into yourself. Withdrawal protects you in the short term, but it feeds a cycle: the less you engage, the more foreign everything feels.

## 3. You idealise your home country

Back home, the weather was better, people were kinder and everything worked. When the present feels hard, the mind looks for comfort in an edited version of the past. The problem is that nothing here can compete with that idealised picture.

## 4. Your body is paying the price

You get ill more often, you sleep badly, you have headaches or stomach problems nobody can quite explain. Chronic stress affects the immune system and sleep. When your body is constantly on alert in an unpredictable environment, it shows.

## 5. You don’t recognise yourself

You used to feel confident and capable; now a phone call or a bill leaves you feeling small. When the context that reflected back who you were disappears, your sense of identity wobbles. It can be rebuilt, but it takes intention, and often support.

> Cultural adjustment isn’t only about learning new customs. It’s also about grieving the version of yourself that made sense in the world you left.

## Families adjust at different speeds

One partner may have a job that brings structure and colleagues, while the other deals with bureaucracy and isolation. Children may pick up the language fast while struggling quietly with belonging. These different rhythms create friction, and children sense it. Talking about adjustment as a family, with compassion for each person’s pace, makes a real difference.

## What helps

- **Lower the bar for yourself.** You are not failing at living abroad; you are going through one of life’s hardest transitions.
- **Build a mixed network:** people who share your experience and local people who can help you understand the new culture.
- **Invest in the language.** Even imperfect skills give you a sense of agency that reduces stress.
- **Keep routines from home.** Familiar habits are an anchor in times of change.
- **Engage with the culture on your own terms.** You don’t have to adopt every custom; find what genuinely resonates.
- **Ask for professional help** if these signs persist. A therapist who understands migration can help you find strategies that fit your situation.

Cultural adjustment isn’t a problem to solve but a process to move through. Noticing what is happening to you is already a step forward.
