When you decided to move abroad as a family, you probably thought about visas, housing and schools. What is harder to see is the emotional landscape your child will have to navigate. Children who grow up between cultures, often called **third culture kids**, face challenges that most parenting books never mention.

## What is a third culture kid?

Sociologist Ruth Hill Useem coined the term in the 1950s for children who spend a significant part of their childhood in a culture different from their parents’. The “third culture” is neither the parents’ nor the host country’s: it is a hybrid identity the child builds from both, plus the experience of living in between.

## Strengths, and their hidden cost

Third culture kids often become adaptable, empathetic and multilingual. But those strengths come with challenges that rarely get named:

- **Rootlessness:** “Where are you from?” has no simple answer.
- **Unresolved grief:** every move means saying goodbye to friends, teachers and familiar places, often silently, while the adults focus on the new adventure.
- **Identity questions:** too foreign at school, too local with family back home.
- **Early maturity** that can hide real emotional needs.
- **Guarded attachments:** after many goodbyes, some children keep friendships at the surface to protect themselves.

## School is more than a new classroom

A new school means a new social code: how friendships form, what is funny, what earns respect. Even children who pick up the language quickly may lack the emotional vocabulary to ask for help or resolve conflicts. And a child who was confident academically may suddenly feel lost under a different teaching system, which affects their self-esteem.

## How parents’ grief affects children

Children are remarkably tuned in to their parents’ emotions, even unspoken ones. You don’t need to hide your feelings; naming them helps. Saying “I miss Grandma today” gives your child permission to feel and express their own loss. What causes harm is tension that is felt but never explained: children may blame themselves or hide their own needs so as not to add to the family’s stress.

> Children don’t need perfect parents. They need parents who are honest about the hard parts of the journey, and who show them it’s safe to feel what they feel.

## When the family adapts at different speeds

Children often integrate faster than adults, and sometimes end up acting as the family’s translator or cultural interpreter. It can seem harmless, but it puts adult responsibilities on young shoulders. A teenager who embraces the local culture may also clash with parents trying to preserve their own.

## Practical ways to help

- **Validate their grief.** Instead of “You’ll make new friends!”, try “Of course you miss them.”
- **Create continuity** with rituals and traditions from home.
- **Talk about identity as something that adds up,** not something split in half: “You carry all these places inside you.”
- **Help them say goodbye well** before a move: photos, contacts, one last visit to favourite places.
- **Stay connected to their previous world** through calls and visits when possible.
- **Watch for changes:** withdrawal, aggression, falling grades, sleep problems or regression.
- **Look for professional support** when needed. A space of their own with a therapist who understands migration can help them say what they find hard to tell you, and that is perfectly healthy.

Growing up between cultures is also a gift. With support, these children often become flexible, empathetic adults who know that belonging is something you can build, not only something you inherit.
